For a **36-year-old lean man whose goal is to gain muscle, strength and look noticeably more muscular**, I’d use a controlled lean-bulk approach. Being 36 doesn’t require a fundamentally different program from someone in their 20s; good exercise selection, progressive overload, nutrition and recovery matter far more.

Your unlimited gym time is useful, but I would **not** turn that into 2-hour workouts. Around **70–100 minutes of lifting** is plenty.

## Weekly structure

| Day | Training |
|---|---|
| Monday | Upper A — chest/back strength emphasis |
| Tuesday | Lower A — squat/hamstring emphasis |
| Wednesday | Recovery + light cardio |
| Thursday | Upper B — shoulders/back/hypertrophy |
| Friday | Lower B — legs/glutes hypertrophy |
| Saturday | Optional arms + shoulders + cardio |
| Sunday | Complete rest |

This gives every major muscle roughly **2 quality training exposures per week**, which is an excellent setup for gaining muscle.

# Wednesday — Recovery

Don't turn the recovery day into another workout.

Do approximately **20–30 minutes of easy Zone-2 cardio**. Incline treadmill walking, cycling, elliptical or rowing are all fine.

You should be able to maintain a conversation during this cardio.

A short mobility session afterward is also fine, particularly for hips, ankles, shoulders and thoracic spine.

# How hard should you train?

This is extremely important.

Use **RIR — Reps In Reserve**.

If you could have done 2 more repetitions when you stopped, that's **2 RIR**.

For most exercises, finish with approximately:

| Exercise type | Target |
|---|---|
| Squats / presses / rows / RDLs | 1–3 RIR |
| Machines | 1–2 RIR |
| Curls / lateral raises / extensions | 0–2 RIR |

You don't need to take squats, deadlifts or bench presses to complete muscular failure.

For something safe like lateral raises or curls, occasionally taking the final set to failure is fine.

# The progression system

This is what actually makes the program work.

Imagine your bench prescription is:

**4 × 5–8**

You start with 60 kg:

Week 1: 8, 7, 7, 6  
Week 2: 8, 8, 7, 7  
Week 3: 8, 8, 8, 7  
Week 4: 8, 8, 8, 8

Now increase the weight, perhaps to **62.5 kg**, and repeat.

Use roughly the same approach for everything.

For upper-body compound movements, increases of around **1–2.5 kg** are often enough. For lower-body exercises, around **2.5–5 kg** may be appropriate. For isolation exercises, use the smallest increment available.

Don't increase weight simply because another week has passed. Increase it because you've earned the progression with better repetitions.

---

# First two weeks

If you're returning after a long break or aren't currently training regularly, **do not start with all the volume above immediately**.

For Weeks 1–2, perform approximately **two working sets per exercise** instead.

Use around **3 RIR**.

Your objective is to learn the exercises and let joints, connective tissue and muscles adapt.

From Week 3 onward, transition into the full program.

# Warm-up

Before every workout, spend about **5–8 minutes** on a treadmill, bike, rower or elliptical.

Then warm up specifically for your first major exercise.

For example, if your working squat is 80 kg:

20 kg × 10  
40 kg × 8  
60 kg × 4  
70 kg × 2  
then working sets at 80 kg.

Warm-up sets **do not count as your working sets**.

You don't need elaborate 20-minute stretching routines before lifting.

# Nutrition — this will determine how much muscle you gain

For a naturally lean person, **food is often the limiting factor rather than the gym**.

Start with a calorie surplus of approximately:

**maintenance calories + 200–300 kcal/day**

Aim for bodyweight to rise approximately:

**0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week.**

If your average morning weight hasn't increased for **2–3 consecutive weeks**, add approximately another **150–200 calories per day**.

## Protein

Aim for:

**1.6–2.2 g protein per kg bodyweight every day.**

Examples:

| Bodyweight | Protein target |
|---:|---:|
| 55 kg | 90–120 g |
| 60 kg | 95–130 g |
| 65 kg | 105–140 g |
| 70 kg | 110–155 g |
| 75 kg | 120–165 g |
| 80 kg | 130–175 g |

You don't need to obsess over the very top of the range. Around **1.8 g/kg/day** is an excellent practical target.

# Indian-style muscle-building diet

There is no special workout requirement because you're Indian, but an Indian diet can be made extremely effective for muscle gain.

A typical day could look something like this:

| Meal | Example |
|---|---|
| Breakfast | 3 eggs + oats + milk/curd + banana |
| Snack | Greek yogurt + fruit + nuts |
| Lunch | Rice/roti + dal + chicken/fish/paneer/tofu + vegetables |
| Pre-workout | Banana + coffee + yogurt |
| Post-workout | Whey protein + fruit |
| Dinner | Rice/roti + chicken/fish/paneer/tofu/soya + vegetables |
| Before bed | Milk or Greek yogurt |

Good high-protein Indian-friendly staples include chicken breast/thigh, eggs, fish, prawns, Greek yogurt, dahi, milk, paneer, tofu, soya chunks, whey, dal, rajma and chana.

For vegetarians, **soya chunks, tofu, dairy and whey** make reaching high protein much easier than relying primarily on dal.

Don't be afraid of rice or roti. For someone trying to gain muscle, carbohydrates are useful because they support hard training.

# Supplements

You don't need a large supplement stack.

The one performance supplement I'd strongly consider is **creatine monohydrate: 3–5 g every day**. Timing doesn't matter much, and you don't need a loading phase.

Whey protein is useful only because it makes hitting your protein target easier; it isn't superior to normal high-quality dietary protein.

Caffeine before training is optional.

Most other bodybuilding supplements are low priority compared with food, training and sleep.

---

# Cardio

Don't eliminate cardio just because you're trying to gain weight.

Do approximately:

**2 × 20–30 minutes Zone-2 cardio per week.**

And stay reasonably active during normal life.

You don't need huge amounts of HIIT while you're actively trying to gain muscle unless you particularly enjoy it.

---

# Sleep and recovery

Target **7.5–9 hours of sleep** whenever possible.

Muscles are stimulated in the gym but adaptation happens during recovery. More training isn't automatically better.

A useful rule is:

**Train → recover → become stronger → train slightly harder.**

Not:

**Train → destroy yourself → remain sore → train again.**

---

# Deloads

You don't need an automatic deload every fourth week.

Consider one after roughly **6–10 hard weeks**, or sooner if several things happen simultaneously: strength starts falling, joints ache more than usual, enthusiasm for training collapses, sleep worsens and your normal weights suddenly feel unusually heavy.

During a deload, reduce your number of sets roughly in half and use **10–20% lighter weights** for about one week.

Then resume normal training.

---

# What to track

The most important thing is your **training log**. For every workout, record exercise, weight, repetitions and sets. Also track morning bodyweight several times per week and use the weekly average rather than reacting to individual measurements.

Progress photos every **4 weeks** under similar lighting are also much more informative than looking in the mirror every day.

For the first 12 weeks, your central objective should be very simple:

**eat enough → hit protein → train consistently → add reps/weight → sleep → repeat.**
